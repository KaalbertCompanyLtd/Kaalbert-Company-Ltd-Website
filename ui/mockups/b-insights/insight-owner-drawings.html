Owner Drawings: The Habit That Quietly Sinks SMEs

EA
Evans Agyemang Financial Control & Compliance · Published 3 August 2026 · 6 min read

Mixing personal spending with business cash is one of the most normal things in an SME — and one of the most quietly expensive. Money moves from the business to the owner and back so often that, after a while, nobody could tell you with confidence whether the business actually made money this year. Not because anyone did anything wrong. Because nothing was ever separated in the first place.

This isn't a judgement. It's the usual starting point for almost every business we see, and it's one of the fastest things to fix once it's named clearly.

"Owner drawings mixed with business cash are normal in an SME — separating them is often the single most useful thing an engagement does."

What it actually looks like

It rarely looks like one big withdrawal. It looks like a fuel purchase paid from the business account, a supplier paid with personal mobile money because the business account was low that week, a family expense covered "just this once" from the till. Each one is small. None of them is dishonest. Together, they make the business's real performance unreadable.

SignalWhat it usually means
Bank balance looks fine, but you can't say if you made a profitDrawings and business costs are mixed in the same account
Sales are up, but there's never more cash than last yearGrowth is being absorbed by untracked personal spending
You can't answer "what do I actually pay myself?"There's no defined owner's salary — just ad hoc withdrawals

Why it matters more than it seems to

Every one of the firm's core offers eventually runs into this. A funding application stalls because the numbers a lender is asked to trust include unexplained personal transactions. A health check flags "cash control" as a weak dimension for exactly this reason. It's rarely the headline problem a founder walks in with — but it's very often the first thing that has to be fixed before anything else can be trusted.

  • Open a separate account for the business, even a basic one — this alone does most of the work.
  • Decide on a fixed, regular amount you pay yourself, and treat anything beyond it as a deliberate decision, not a habit.
  • Record every transfer between you and the business, even informally — a notebook entry beats no record at all.
